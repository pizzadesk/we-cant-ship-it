WE CAN'T SHIP IT -- Global Voice Guide
=====================================

This is the source of truth for the game's narrative flavor.

If you are writing cards, reviews, popups, console messages, jank names,
prospect hints, or event text, follow this guide first.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
CORE IDEA
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The game is about a studio shipping something too ambitious, slightly
broken, and weirdly sincere.

The voice should feel:
  - deadpan
  - observant
  - specific
  - affectionate toward failure
  - sincere about accidental beauty

The voice should NOT feel:
  - random
  - loud for its own sake
  - cruel toward the studio
  - smug toward the player
  - generic game-marketing copy
  - placeholder debug output


------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE GOLD STANDARD
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The feature card descriptions are the target voice.

They usually work like this:
  1. State the system plainly.
  2. Add one concrete complication or contradiction.
  3. End on a dry sting that makes the failure memorable.

Example shape:
  "Bodies collapse with physical accuracy. Two stack at the doorway.
   One slides down the hill until you leave the area."

That rhythm should guide the rest of the game.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
UNIVERSAL RULES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Be concrete.
   Name the system, the bug, the consequence, the ritual, the object,
   the room, the time of day. Avoid vague phrasing like "things got weird"
   when you can say what actually happened.

2. Treat jank as culture, not just failure.
   The best lines are about what people did with the accident:
   preserved it, argued about it, routed around it, mythologized it,
   built a challenge around it, wrote lore about it.

3. Stay straight-faced.
   The writing can be funny, but the narrator should not sound like it is
   begging for laughs.

4. Never mock the player for caring.
   If players turn a bug into a ritual, archive, doctrine, pilgrimage,
   or competitive format, the game should treat that as real meaning.

5. Avoid empty "lol random" absurdity.
   Weirdness must come from systems colliding, not from unrelated jokes.

6. Avoid generic industry language unless it is the joke.
   Words like synergy, innovation, immersion, content, engagement, and
   polish are weak unless used very deliberately.

7. End strong.
   Good lines often close on a crisp reversal, verdict, or aftershock.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT THE GAME BELIEVES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The game believes these things sincerely:

  - broken games can still be loved
  - accidents can reveal authorship
  - communities finish the meaning of shipped work
  - pressure deforms the work, but can also make it specific
  - a game can fail technically and still succeed culturally

If a line contradicts that worldview, it is probably wrong.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
SURFACE-BY-SURFACE RULES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. FEATURE CARDS

  Voice:
    concise, system-first, dry, sharply observed

  Use:
    concrete mechanics, visible consequences, one memorable sting

  Avoid:
    internet slang, over-explaining, sentimental phrasing


2. STUDIO CONSOLE

  Voice:
    in-universe production diary

  It should sound like:
    the team, the room, the board, the schedule, the morale, the cost,
    the decision that just became real

  It should not sound like:
    telemetry, debug logging, button labels, or menu state output


3. POPUPS / ENDINGS / RECAPS

  Voice:
    larger, more reflective, still restrained

  These lines can be:
    more sincere, mythic, or conclusive than card text

  But they still must:
    stay specific and earned


4. OFFERS / DILEMMAS / THRESHOLD EVENTS

  Voice:
    production pressure made legible

  Every choice should feel like:
    a tradeoff the studio could plausibly argue about in a room

  Never frame one option as obviously correct.


5. SIGNATURE JANK

  Voice:
    named folklore

  Names and descriptions should suggest:
    a real emergent phenomenon players would remember, clip, route,
    defend, archive, or worship

  Good signature jank feels:
    specific enough to picture, strange enough to retell


6. REVIEWS

  Voice:
    outlet-specific, but always tied to the shipped game

  Rule:
    reviewers are allowed to sound different from each other,
    but they must still react to the actual shipped accident, mechanic,
    or cultural afterlife of the build

  Reviewers do not describe abstract genre promises.
  They describe what players are actually seeing, doing, preserving,
  arguing about, or recommending.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
MEMEOLOGY RULES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Recurring community myth should usually fall into one or more of these
families:

  - patch_defense
      Players argue the bug must never be fixed.

  - pilgrimage_site
      Players visit a place, state, route, or moment because it became
      culturally important.

  - sacred_timing
      A bug acquires a ritual hour, condition, or timing window.

  - theorycraft_canon
      Players write explanations, doctrine, spreadsheets, or arguments
      until the accident hardens into shared truth.

  - lore_archive
      Players document the bug like history, canon, or sacred text.

  - build_cult
      Players reorganize how they play around the accident.

  - competitive_ritual
      Players turn the accident into routes, categories, tournaments,
      or challenge rules.

Horses are one meme lineage, not the whole game.

------------------------------------------------------------------------
GOOD VS BAD
------------------------------------------------------------------------

GOOD:
  "Quest markers persist after completion in dense world areas. Players
   reported following a ghost. Fan fiction resulted."

WHY IT WORKS:
  specific system, concrete consequence, cultural afterlife


BAD:
  "A weird bug created some funny emergent gameplay moments."

WHY IT FAILS:
  vague, generic, no image, no consequence, no point of view


GOOD:
  "The room chose Venture Funding. The runway relaxed. The game did not."

WHY IT WORKS:
  sounds like studio diary, not UI telemetry


BAD:
  "Choice accepted: Venture Funding"

WHY IT FAILS:
  placeholder system output


------------------------------------------------------------------------
QUICK CHECKLIST
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before you save new text, ask:

  - Can I picture what is happening?
  - Does this sound like the game, not a template?
  - Is the humor dry rather than noisy?
  - Is the line affectionate toward the mess?
  - Does this describe a real consequence or community response?
  - Could this be mistaken for placeholder/debug text?

If the answer to the last question is yes, rewrite it.
